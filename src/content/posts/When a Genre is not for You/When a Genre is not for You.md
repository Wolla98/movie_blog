# Horror


Recently I went out to watch *Weapons* (2025) in theaters. It was a movie that got shockingly high critical reviews so needless to say I was a bit excited to watch it. I was a bit worried because it was a horror movie though. When it comes to horror movies, I'm a bit out of my depth. I will admit, most of the time there's a horror movie on screen I've not been impressed by it. Maybe it's because I'm cynical or overly critical, but I've always felt a bit bored of horror. So, as I sat down with a bucket of popcorn for the film, I waited with apprehension and suspense (hey that's like horror!).


Coming out of the theater, I was sorely disappointed by the movie. I felt that the pacing style that they used for the first half of the movie was interesting for the mystery but felt more like padding for time than any meaningful character building. The latter half of the movie felt much better paced, but in turn played the entire mystery as a straight story which destroyed the appeal of the first half. The characters were so uninteresting that I couldn't even muster any feelings at their misfortunes. It was a pretty terrible movie, with only a few redeeming qualities (I liked some of the shots and action bits).


That got me thinking though, do I just not get horror movies? Like on a personal level, am I unable to interface emotionally with these movies? When I watch movies, I find it hard to get emotional on a personal level with the characters and the themes. I rarely feel sympathy or sadness for the characters more than a surface level. I don't think I've ever been personally scared, afraid, angry, or anything of that sort when watching movies. That's a bit strange for a critic, isn't it? I mean, movies arguably have three main purposes: to make you think, to make you feel, and to entertain you. How can I come to terms with the fact that it's really hard for me to interact with the "feeling" portion of movies?


I can count on one hand the number of movies that genuinely got me feeling personal strong emotions. To me, it's enough for the characters themselves to feel the emotions. After all, the events of the story aren't happening to me, it's happening to the characters. I think this is one place that horror falls flat for me. The characters usually end up being pretty flat. I think it's a result of needing a large cast so you can have a few people die along with the fact that the "main character" usually ends up being a singular antagonist or protagonist. It's not inherently bad to have flat characters, especially if they are useful to the plot. However, if you want me to feel anything when a character dies, I'm going to need a little more than a character quirk and their relationship to a more important character. In *Weapons*, there's a character who gets shot by another character. These two characters have had a hot-and-cold relationship but they clearly have a lot of history together. Yet, the character didn't feel like they were affected by it at all. And I obviously didn't feel affected by it either, in part because the character didn't seem like they cared. It's hard to care about the misfortune of people I frankly don't give a crap about.


I think the biggest sin that these horror movies have is that they present strong ideas, but always seem to ruin it by overexplaining. I think this is another personal thing, but when there's screaming monsters and ghouls my brain goes "Ah, the metaphor would've been better than this but I guess we need monsters". I remember watching *Sinners* (2025) and being so enthralled in the drama and world set up in the first half of the movie. Then, I remember being less interested in the second half, despite the admittedly strong monster-as-a-metaphor. I honestly think if the movie was not a horror but played the drama from the first half straight all the way to the end it would end up as a strong 4.5 or 5.


This is where my real gripes with *Weapons* begins. It's clear from the beginning (and the title) that the movie is setting up a comparison to real life school shootings. One of my favorite scenes is right at the beginning with the group of angry parents yelling at the teacher. It had my mind swimming with thoughts about the direction of the story. Would it be a dramatic look into how a group of angry people can be whipped into a frenzy and become more horrifying than any monster? Would it be an exploration of how people react and cope with a tragedy they can not understand and how they lash out at others or close in on themselves? It presented an **idea** that was unfortunately better than anything that the movie had to offer. No, it turns out the citizens were just terrible people from the start. That idea about the horror of a seemingly random tragedy that people are powerless to stop? Nah, it's actually just a witch that can be killed. Even the school shooting metaphor falls apart because the initial tragedy was actually fully orchestrated and carried out by the witch. The kids had pretty much nothing to do with it. Maybe it would've worked if it had a bit more characterization from the witch, but she's just a generic evil bad guy who wants to do evil. The comedy also felt like a rejection of this thematic idea, although unintentionally. It felt like at its core, the movie felt too insecure to let a heavy idea really "sit" with the viewer. That maybe if we went too long without a joke or slapstick comedy that we might think about the themes a little too hard and not like it. Laughing became a form of catharsis and therefore a convenient point at which to stop thinking about the themes of the movie. I sincerely think that the ending of a film can, by itself, raise or lower the final score by a full point or more. So, believe me when I say that I found the ending to be very disappointing.

And I think that's the core issue I have with horror movies. Rarely do they ever live up to the **idea** that they present. There always seems to be a hesitance to commit fully, to really touch the core of human fear. The two horror movies I actually like, *Sinners* and *The Thing* (1982), do the opposite. *Sinners* sets up its themes in the first half wonderfully and actually ties in its core ideas (black spaces being destroyed and celebrations of culture being regarded as "sinful") into the monster. *The Thing*, one of my favorite movies of all time, leans into its core idea of imposters and distrust so hard that even in the ending are we, the audience, denied total catharsis. Side note, that's also why I hate "The Thing Ending Explained" videos because thematically, it doesn't matter who's actually the Thing.


It seems all too familiar to The Backrooms horror that was popular on the internet a few years back. It started off touching something deeply rooted in basic human fear. It's fear of something familiar yet different in a way that is unexplainable. The fear that comes from feeling like you recognize a place or have seen something before but can't figure out where you've seen it (and what else you've forgotten). It's a classic horror formula: take something familiar and change it just enough to be uncomfortable. But then, as happens for all good things on the internet, people who didn't understand the reason behind the horror, *cough children cough*, starting adding a bunch of explanations and lore like they're Game Theory solving "The Truth Behind The Backrooms". Suddenly the appeal of that original horror was gone. That feeling of uneasiness of being in a place that's usually full of people but instead it's empty? Gone. It's now a hundred levels of monsters and ghouls. Have fun.


So, what comes next? I think I'll still stick around to watch horror movies when they come up. I don't think the whole genre is doomed but that I just need to seek out the really good ones. In truth, I don't really like giving reviews or scores on horror similarly to comedy movies. There's something that feels so personal and emotional about horror and comedy that it doesn't feel right to take my usual position of critical analysis against them. Who knows though, maybe I'll find a smash hit that changes my mind about the whole genre. I'll be happy to keep an open mind moving into the next one.
